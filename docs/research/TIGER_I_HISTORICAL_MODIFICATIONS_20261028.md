# Historical Tiger I modifications for physical progression

A convincing Tiger I progression can grow around a machine that becomes more dependable, easier to observe from, better equipped and more personally familiar. There were genuine engine, running gear, sight, turret and equipment changes. There was also a continuous stream of small reliability corrections. The historical record supports a substantial workshop game, but it does not support an endless sequence of larger engines, more powerful guns and thicker armor fitted by any crew anywhere.

The strongest major upgrade is the **Maybach HL210 to HL230 conversion**, if the starting vehicle actually has the earlier engine. Other strong candidates are a complete steel-tyred wheel conversion, replacement optics, improved cooling and fuel-system components, and useful stowage and observation equipment. Replacing a damaged transmission or rebuilding an engine can be a major accomplishment without inventing a superior transmission or a horsepower bonus.

This is a bounded inventory of important documented changes and useful smaller corrections, not a claim to enumerate every production alteration or undocumented unit improvisation. “Early,” “mid” and “late” describe overlapping configurations; they should not become three clean upgrade levels. A factory introduction date does not mean every older tank received the feature.

## How to distinguish the evidence

- **Contemporary record:** a wartime manual, technical report or drawing. The Panzer Elmito links provide Spanish translations with links to German facsimiles. A report can establish that a unit tried something without establishing universal adoption.
- **Physical evidence:** surviving hardware, contemporary photographs and measured surveys. David Byrden's articles identify original drawings and direct surveys where available. His inferred dates and explanations remain interpretations rather than retrofit orders.
- **Museum account:** useful for established configuration changes and for distinguishing wartime hardware from modern restoration. A running museum vehicle can contain later or newly made parts.

Dates below identify the documented period or approximate introduction, rather than an exact availability date for a fictional campaign. Installation scale is a game-design classification, not a historical repair instruction.

## Engine cooling and running gear

| Change | Historical status and evidence | Appropriate role in the game |
|---|---|---|
| HL210 replaced by HL230 | Production change in 1943 and an explicitly documented conversion. German sketch **SK3426**, approved 29 March 1943 and amended in 1944, covers an engine upgrade with associated equipment. The surrounding fan drives, regulator and plumbing changed too. [Engine room equipment](https://tiger1.info/EN/Engine-room-equipment.html) | A major workshop milestone for an early tank. Treat it as a package of compatible systems. An existing HL230-equipped tank cannot receive this historical upgrade a second time. |
| Revised fan drive arrangement with the HL230 | The old shared rear gearbox and clutch arrangement gave way to two fan gearboxes. This is supported by German equipment drawings and surviving vehicles, not just external resemblance. [Engine room equipment](https://tiger1.info/EN/Engine-room-equipment.html) | Large, visible components with a clear connection to the engine conversion. A good use of the detailed engine bay. |
| Revised coolant pipes and electrical equipment | SK3426 includes revised pipes and replacement of the regulator; its 6 March 1944 amendment specifies thicker pipes. [Engine room equipment](https://tiger1.info/EN/Engine-room-equipment.html) | Supporting work that makes the major conversion credible. Group minor fittings into a manageable assembly task. |
| HL230 internal reliability corrections | A **15 December 1943** engineering review records copper sealing rings replacing aluminium, corrected bearing manufacture, stronger crankshaft-end and damper measures, a 2500 rpm restriction, and an additional support bearing entering production. Some other suggested changes were rejected. [Contemporary engineering review](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/panther/informes/HL%20230_15_12_1943_E.html) | A rebuilt engine with documented revisions can be more dependable. This is not evidence for a series of performance-piston kits. The report concerns the engine family; a particular Tiger installation still needs its parts configuration established. |
| Higher fan speed in service | A **21 August 1944** report from s.Pz.Abt.502 says fan speed had been increased, but cooling remained inadequate during unusually demanding hot-weather marches. It does not establish a universal named upgrade kit. [Unit technical report](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/PzAbt-502_210844_E.html) | A possible researched cooling revision, with a finite thermal benefit. Do not promise a specific interchangeable fan model or a horsepower gain from this report alone. |
| Air filters changed with the engine | The HL210 had three oil-bath filter canisters; the HL230 had two larger ones. Original engine drawings, the Tigerfibel and surviving assemblies document the difference. [Engine air filters](https://tiger1.info/EN/Engine-air-filters.html) | Useful diagnosis, servicing and replacement. Two filters are not automatically “better” than three; they belong to different engine arrangements. |
| External Feifel precleaners added and revised | Detachable precleaners addressed dusty environments. A simplified form appeared in 1943; the system was later dropped from production. Surviving hardware documents both forms. Crews did not always carry the removable units. [Feifel survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/Feifel-air-precleaners.html) | An environmental equipment choice for an appropriate early vehicle, with external ducts and storage consequences. Not a universal late-war performance upgrade. |
| Rubber-tyred road wheels replaced by resilient steel-tyred wheels | Introduced in production from **February 1944**. The new design still contained rubber cushioning, but internally. Complete sets could and did replace the earlier wheels; mixing types was forbidden except in emergencies. There was no universal retrofit programme. [Tank Museum wheel study](https://tankmuseum.org/article/tiger-wheels) | One of the strongest physical upgrades: a major workshop conversion that improves durability and changes the vehicle's appearance. Availability should matter. |
| Wheel layout reduced with the new wheel type | The steel-tyred arrangement used sixteen wheels per side instead of twenty-four, and no longer required removing the outer row for transport tracks. [Tank Museum wheel study](https://tankmuseum.org/article/tiger-wheels) | A complete assembly change with a practical servicing benefit, not eight optional wheels arbitrarily removed for speed. |
| Later cleated combat tracks | A later track pattern added integral cleats; surviving measurements and German/Russian technical drawings distinguish the patterns. [Track and vehicle height study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Roof-height.html) | Better terrain-specific equipment, with the actual pattern and availability matched to the tank. Avoid importing Panzer III/IV “Winterketten” extensions merely because the name sounds appropriate. |
| Removable ice cleats | A **14 April 1943** workshop report confirms actual use, rapid wear and loss, and limited effectiveness on ice. Its proposed improved winter links were a request, not proof of adoption. [Winter mobility report](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/tiger_i_14_04_1943_E.html) | A situational preparation choice, with honest limitations. Do not turn an unsuccessful trial into an unconditional traction upgrade. |
| Combat and transport track sets | These were separate working configurations, not successive technology levels. A **28 September 1943** order discusses unit responsibility for maintaining and returning transport tracks. [Transport track order](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/tiger_i_28_09_1943_E.html) | Appropriate for an occasional rail-transfer chapter; repetitive full track conversion should not become mandatory upkeep between every encounter. |

The distinction between **condition** and **design** matters. A damaged radiator replaced by an intact example restores cooling. A historically revised assembly changes the design. Both can feel rewarding, but the game should explain them differently. Likewise, a clean, complete, correctly matched donor engine may be more valuable than a theoretically newer but damaged one.

Modern restorations demonstrate the work involved but require careful interpretation. Tiger 131 was fitted with an HL230P30 taken from a Tiger II during restoration, with specially made fan gearboxes. That is evidence of the restoration's engineering, not proof that an intact Panther or Tiger II engine was a routine drop-in Tiger I upgrade. [Tank Museum restoration account](https://tankmuseum.org/article/hull-tiger-131)

## Observation protection and crew equipment

| Change | Historical status and evidence | Appropriate role in the game |
|---|---|---|
| Lower commander's cupola with seven periscopes | Part of the redesigned turret introduced in **mid-1943**. It changed the roof opening, protection, hatch and observation arrangement. [New turret survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/New-turret-external.html) | Valuable for cupola play, but treat a conversion as a major structural or replacement-turret project. The existence of the new cupola does not establish a simple interchangeable hatch kit. |
| Cupola machine-gun ring | Added around the newer cupola, with subsequent changes to the ring and hatch swing. [New turret survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/New-turret-external.html) | An exposed optional station and equipment choice, with a real location on the tank. It should not silently become an extra autonomous weapon. |
| Loader's periscope | Added in **March 1943**, then redesigned for the newer turret. It gave the loader a way to observe shot effects. [Manual-based periscope study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Loaders-periscope.html) | A particularly good crew-related improvement: it changes what that person can see and communicate through gesture or a restrained visual cue. |
| TZF9b binocular sight replaced by TZF9c monocular sight | A real **1944** configuration change. The turret manual includes provision for plugging the unused mantlet opening; a plugged example survives at Saumur. The use of plugs is firm evidence, while the extent of retrofitting older tanks is less certain. [Telescope plug](https://tiger1.info/EN/Telescope-plug.html), [calibration documentation](https://tiger1.info/EN/Calibration-target.html) | A substantial optics project rather than a purchased accuracy percentage. Match the sight, mount and mantlet configuration. Verify exact optical settings against the selected sight's manual before promising a zoom progression. |
| Reinforced mantlet around the sight apertures | The turret manual and associated geometry show the earlier thinner area and the later protective bulge. [Mantlet aperture study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Telescope-holes.html) | A real protective design improvement, most plausibly obtained through a replacement compatible mantlet assembly. This does not justify arbitrary armor plates across moving weapon parts. |
| Turret roof increased from 25 mm to 40 mm | A **1944 production** change. The factory drawing shows different roof construction, preserving internal dimensions while adding thickness outside. [Factory drawing study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Turret-roof.html) | A new roof or turret configuration, not a demonstrated field bolt-on layer. Keep it out of a casual garage menu unless the story supplies a capable heavy workshop. |
| Revised loader's hatch for the thicker roof | The later hatch was countersunk into the thicker roof. [Surviving hatch detail](https://tiger1.info/EN/Hachette-loaders-hatch.html) | A compatibility dependency of the roof change, not a separate protection token. |
| Three turret-roof crane sockets | Present on final-production vehicles and documented on the surviving Saumur Tiger. [Crane socket survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/Takom-Pilze.html) | A serviceability improvement and visible sign of workshop capability. Sockets alone do not provide the crane, lifting equipment or a safe place to work. |
| Turret ventilation revised | The new turret moved the fan above the source of fumes and used a different housing and cover. [New turret survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/New-turret-external.html) | A possible visibility and crew-comfort consequence, if the game actually represents fumes. Avoid a benefit that exists only in a menu. |
| Pistol-port and hatch details changed | The new turret simplified its pistol port and side-hatch edges; these were structural production refinements. [New turret survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/New-turret-external.html) | Important for a coherent model. Most are poor standalone rewards. |
| Handholds added inside the turret | Early surviving tanks differ; later drawings specify the handles. [Original drawing and vehicle survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/Roof-handles.html) | Small physical improvements can communicate care for the crew without becoming another upgrade tier. |
| Turret stowage bins added and developed | A purpose-built rear bin addressed storage needs; its shape and position had to preserve the escape hatch, engine ventilation and commander's view. [Measured bin survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/Standard-turret-bin.html) | An excellent early workshop project: meaningful capacity, visible belongings and personal ownership. Clearance should constrain the design. |
| Spare-track carriers on turret sides | Original drawings and photos show several layouts, including changes needed to keep the engine hatch accessible. [Track holder study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Turret-track-holders.html) | Strong basis for attaching salvaged compatible track links and maintaining a practical spare supply. Coverage is local; a track link is not automatically equivalent to armor plate of the same overall depth. |
| Zimmerit coating | A real anti-magnetic-mine measure used during 1943–44, including field application. It was not extra ballistic armor. [Tank Museum Zimmerit account](https://tankmuseum.org/article/zimmerit) | A historically specific treatment or visual chapter detail. Do not award shell resistance for it, or invent widespread magnetic-mine enemies to justify an upgrade branch. |

## Armament smoke and communications

| Change | Historical status and evidence | Appropriate role in the game |
|---|---|---|
| Early turret-side smoke launchers and internal controls | Fitted in early production and retrofitted; the controls were relocated as turret layouts changed. Production installation ended in **June 1943**. [Manual and hardware study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Smoke-grenade-triggers.html) | A credible early equipment project. Do not combine every historical smoke system into one supposedly standard late Tiger. |
| Hull-mounted S-mine system | Brackets and wiring appeared before all vehicles received launchers. The five-position system and its removal during 1943 are documented through photographs, manuals and surviving tanks. Command vehicles differed. [S-mine brackets](https://tiger1.info/EN/S-mine-brackets.html), [system survey](https://tiger1.info/EN/S-mine-controls.html) | An optional period configuration with danger to nearby friendly personnel. Its presence should not be inferred just from empty mounting brackets. |
| Roof-mounted Nahverteidigungswaffe | The **7 September 1944 Tiger E turret manual** describes the close-defense and smoke/signal equipment. That establishes a late configuration, not that every earlier Tiger received it. [Manual catalogue](https://bushmakow.com/library/177-d-656_22-armored-combat-vehicle-tiger-design-e-a-armored-command-vehicle-tiger-design-e-operating-instruction-f-tower/), [German manual transcription](https://tigertank181.com/45_Nahverteidigungswaffe.htm) | A possible late turret capability. Installation and ammunition availability should be tied to a compatible turret; no instant universal retrofit. |
| Revised muzzle-brake form | Large and small forms are documented. Wartime French-service photographs of the surviving Saumur Tiger show the large form on a tank expected to carry the small one; replacement is the researcher's interpretation. [Muzzle-brake study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Muzzle-brake.html), [Saumur example](https://tiger1.info/EN/9540-Colmar.html) | A visible assembly variation or replacement job. No measured recoil, blast or penetration advantage between these forms is established here. |
| Gun repair and replacement components | The Tiger carried an armourer's tools/spares box; photographs also show gun removal for repairs and replacement-looking assemblies. [Armourer's box](https://tiger1.info/EN/KwK-spares-box.html), [wartime replacement evidence](https://tiger1.info/EN/Tunisian-RAL8020.html) | Repairing or replacing a worn or damaged KwK36 barrel/assembly is a strong workshop event. The reward is restored serviceability and confidence, not an invented calibre increase. |
| Sight and weapon alignment | The turret manual specifies calibration references for the main armament and hull MG. [Calibration documentation](https://tiger1.info/EN/Calibration-target.html) | A short, legible validation sequence after installation can make the player's work matter. Avoid turning it into a lengthy real-world gunnery procedure. |
| Additional receiver for platoon leaders | The ordinary Fu5 arrangement could include a Fu2 receiver, illustrated in the Tigerfibel. [Radio wiring and Tigerfibel illustration](https://tiger1.info/EN/Radio-wiring.html) | A modest communications configuration. With no spoken dialogue, its benefit needs an actual visual or written information channel. |
| Command Tiger radio arrangement | Specialized vehicles carried the Fu5 in the turret and a Fu8 or Fu7 in the hull, with corresponding antenna and equipment changes. [Command antenna study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Command-antennas.html), [original drawing references for equipment](https://tiger1.info/EN/US004-transformers.html) | A role conversion, not simply a longer-range radio bolted into the same free space. It competes with the ordinary interior arrangement. |

The production Tiger I retained the **8.8 cm KwK36 L/56**. The **KwK43 L/71** belongs to the Tiger II and uses different ammunition; the Tank Museum's physical comparison makes the incompatibility clear. A Tiger I with a KwK43 should be treated as an alternate-history redesign requiring a newly justified vehicle configuration, not a historical barrel upgrade. [Tank Museum gun and ammunition comparison](https://tankmuseum.org/article/same-size-different-round)

The user's proposed subtle muzzle, barrel and mantlet variants could preserve the Tiger's silhouette. The historical foundation is strongest for the **two muzzle-brake forms, reinforced mantlet, changed sight opening and replacement serviceable gun parts**. Recoil feel, dust, blast and settling time could distinguish fictional variants, but the sources above do not establish a historical ranking or numerical gains. No distinct sequence of more powerful Tiger I barrel types has been verified here. Do not import the Tiger II's barrel-development story into the KwK36.

A particularly useful existing component is the **protective sleeve in front of the mantlet**. Original-manual and surviving-hardware research explains that damage to the exposed sliding surface could obstruct recoil. This gives the sleeve a readable function in a local damage model without inventing a larger gun. [Protective sleeve study](https://tiger1.info/EN/Mantlet-sleeve.html)

## Proven smaller improvements and repairs

The **20 January 1943 OKH/In 6 report** is unusually useful because it separates defects, current fixes and intended future changes. It records or orders improvements to steering valves, final-drive seals, brake linkage, cooling hose clamps, fuel connections, track-pin retention, heat protection and exhaust covers. It also discusses stronger suspension-arm material, lifting/support features and heater sealing. Some items are manufacturing corrections, others actual retrofit arrangements; they should not all be sold as already available upgrades on that date. [Contemporary report](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/tiger_i_20_01_1943_E.html)

These improvements can be grouped into **substantial workshop jobs**: cooling assembly, fuel-system overhaul, running-gear repair, steering overhaul and electrical repair. The player can expose the affected assembly, identify a fault, remove a substantial part, install its replacement and observe a clear test result. Individual minor fasteners need not each demand a separate action.

A **22 January 1944** report records a unit-made modification to an oil-filler arrangement intended to reduce oil escaping onto hot exhaust components. It also reports defects in newly delivered engines, steering equipment, starters and fan drives. That supports local problem solving and pre-service inspection; it does not support treating every new replacement as flawless. [Technical condition report](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/PzAbt-503_22_01_1944_E.html)

Routine overhaul can have real narrative weight. The August 1944 s.Pz.Abt.502 report describes a long maintenance pause enabling running-gear rebuilding, cleaning and adjustment that could not be completed during operations. Its later failures show how operating demands could overwhelm those gains. In a game, this supports spaced workshops and visibly accumulated condition rather than a constant random breakdown timer. [Unit report](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/PzAbt-502_210844_E.html)

## Repairs in the field and the meaning of local damage

**Inspection anywhere is a strong proposal; unrestricted heavy repair anywhere would be a deliberate abstraction.** An April 1943 US intelligence account distinguishes repair sections travelling with tank units from workshops equipped with power, cranes and welding apparatus. Vehicles exceeding local repair capacity were recovered. This is a contemporary account of German organization, with the limits of wartime intelligence; its quoted hour limits should not become universal Tiger repair times. [Intelligence Bulletin: maintenance and recovery](https://coulthart.lonesentry.com/articles/tankmaint/index.html)

The practical distinction is capability, not a special building. A well-equipped mobile workshop can work outdoors. A large empty garage without parts, lifting equipment or skilled help cannot perform every overhaul. A postwar US Army study based on German experience describes the Vienna repair facility needing stronger lifting provision when Tigers and Panthers arrived. [German Tank Maintenance in World War II, page 28](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo81652/pdf/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo81652.pdf)

For planning, distinguish three outcomes: **a temporary intervention that enables escape; a replacement that restores a specific function; and a thorough workshop repair**. The game can let the player attempt work wherever the necessary access, tools, parts and help exist. Workshops remain valuable because they supply those capabilities. Reducing time and letting one player perform several specialists' work are reasonable abstractions to state openly.

An embedded fragment should not automatically disable its host component. In 2025, the Tank Museum found shrapnel inside a Tiger 131 wheel despite repeated restoration and years of operation. Its exact origin was uncertain. That is a concrete example of retained battle debris coexisting with working machinery. [Museum discovery report](https://tankmuseum.org/article/tiger-shrapnel-discovery/)

Conversely, extracting an obstruction does not repair every consequence of the hit. Tiger 131's restoration exposed damage to its gun-balance assembly associated with an impact elsewhere on the mantlet. The museum also documents a replaced loader's hatch and superficial damage deliberately left intact. These observations support separate states for obstruction, deformation, broken components and cosmetic scars. [Museum battle-damage account](https://tankmuseum.org/article/part-vii-battle-damage)

A **plate patch** should likewise have a stated purpose: covering an opening, protecting exposed equipment or restoring a damaged structural area are different jobs. A visible patch is not evidence that the original armor's ballistic resistance has returned. That equivalence is not established by the sources inspected here. The local damage model should retain the actual material and condition rather than reset the entire plate because an interaction finished.

## Salvaged armor and substantial component swaps

**Salvage is strongly compatible with the concept; arbitrary armor stacking needs a fiction label.** The verified track carriers establish spare-link attachment. They do not establish a general Tiger I programme of cutting plates off destroyed enemy tanks and welding them onto the hull. The 40 mm roof and reinforced mantlet demonstrate real improvements to protection, but those are purpose-designed structural components.

For a fictional salvaged-plate mechanic, the proposed rules should preserve the existing simulation's physical character:

- Each piece protects the place it actually covers. Openings, edges and uncovered areas remain relevant.
- Material quality, condition, orientation and attachment matter; nominal thicknesses are not simply added into a universal protection value.
- Weight and interference with hatches, observation, cooling and moving assemblies create costs.
- The workshop's lifting and fabrication capability limits what can be fitted. The tank does not become universally upgradeable because the player carries a welder.

Those are design constraints for a deliberate extension, not a claim that this was a standard wartime Tiger practice. The most historically grounded first version would emphasize **replacement components, spare tracks, storage, protection for vulnerable accessories, and a small number of explicitly fictional plate arrangements**.

| Requested component idea | Historically grounded foundation | What would require more evidence or declared fiction |
|---|---|---|
| Entire engine | HL210 to HL230 conversion; serviceable replacement engine; rebuilt later engine with documented reliability corrections. | A succession of ever larger Tiger I engines or effortless swapping of all HL230 variants. |
| Transmission | Replacement or overhaul of the existing transmission and steering equipment; documented repairs and associated improved seals/controls. | A verified in-service alternate transmission offering extra speed, gears or torque capacity. No such upgrade branch is established here. |
| Pistons and internal engine parts | Repair and replacement are credible; the contemporary HL230 review documents specific reliability revisions elsewhere in the engine. | A historical Tiger performance-piston ladder. Different pistons cannot be assumed compatible or beneficial simply because they are newer. |
| Radiator and fans | Replacement of damaged cooling assemblies; revised fan drive layout with the engine conversion; unit evidence of increased fan speed. | An exact series of universally interchangeable “better fans” or radiators with known quantified gains. |
| Gun | Serviceable replacement KwK36 components and sight/alignment work. | A historical KwK43 conversion for the ordinary Tiger I. |
| Optics | Correct replacement optics and the actual TZF9b/TZF9c configurations. | Every attractive sight from a donor tank fitting the Tiger, or undocumented night-vision and stabilization upgrades. |

## Special conversions and proposals that should stay separate

The **Sturmtiger** was a real radical conversion using refurbished Tiger I chassis, but it became a different assault vehicle with a different role. It is not a plausible final gun level while preserving the player's familiar Tiger interior. [Tank Museum wheel and chassis account](https://tankmuseum.org/article/tiger-wheels), [Sturmtiger role and weapon](https://tankmuseum.org/article/sturmtigers_fire_power/)

A **10 October 1943** s.Pz.Abt.503 report describes using a badly damaged Tiger as a recovery vehicle. It establishes a specific unit improvisation. It should not be conflated with every photographed crane-equipped vehicle later called a “Bergetiger,” whose exact purpose can be disputed. [Contemporary unit report](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/informes/tiger_i_teil-iv_10_10_1943_E.html)

A surviving drawing proposes a different driver's hatch, but the design was not used on production Tigers. It is a useful example of how an authentic drawing can describe a proposal rather than delivered equipment. [Driver hatch proposal](https://tiger1.info/EN/Proposed-hull-hatches.html)

The catalogue also lists a February 1943 EM09 rangefinder trial report. Its presence establishes a research lead, not a standard rangefinder upgrade or a successful operational system. [Tiger I document catalogue](https://www.panzer-elmito.org/tanques/tiger_i/indice.html)

Smaller production details still need individual verification before a definitive vehicle model is frozen: idler diameter, sprocket and wheel fasteners, turret-ring guards, headlamp locations, travel locks, escape-hatch revisions, waterproofing deletion, fuel-tank shielding, extra ammunition stowage and tool arrangements. Many are known configuration differences, but a precise parts-level schedule should follow the chosen starting chassis and campaign date. They should not all become mandatory progression systems.

## Recommended planning direction

Start by choosing the tank's **initial mechanical configuration**. An early Tiger offers several genuine design changes; a later Tiger offers a stronger restoration-and-customization arc, but fewer historical upgrades remain ahead of it. Starting with an already complete late tank and promising every historical improvement would force contradictions.

Use a small number of spaced, recognizable workshops. Their value comes from the assemblies they can handle, the parts available and what the next journey demands. A workshop with lifting equipment can make an engine job possible; a later stop might offer a scarce intact sight, wheel set or rebuilt transmission. Those should be memorable projects with physical results, not an exhaustive checklist of every historical revision.

For the first planning pass, prioritize five projects: **restore cooling; repair or replace a major drivetrain assembly; improve observation; fit practical stowage and spares; complete one historically justified major conversion**. Let the player retain and recognize the old component, compare it with the replacement, and see the consequences on the next outing. This connects mechanical work to the tank's continuing story while leaving room for the player to care about the crew doing that work alongside them.
